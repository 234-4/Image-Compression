How to Make Photos Smaller in MB: 5 Easy Ways

Published October 27, 2026 · 4 min read

Your photo is 6 MB, and the upload page wants it under 2. Or your phone says storage is full, and every new picture takes another few megabytes. Either way you need the same thing: a smaller file that still looks good. Here are five reliable ways to make photos smaller in MB, from quickest to most flexible.

What makes a photo big?

File size depends on three things: pixel dimensions, quality setting and format. A phone photo can be 4,000 pixels wide, far more than most screens or forms need. Reducing any of the three shrinks the file, and combining them gives the biggest drop. Some tools ask for kilobytes instead, and as a rough guide, 1 MB is about 1,000 KB.

Way 1: Compress the photo online

This is the fastest method and works on any device.

  1. Open the image compressor and choose your JPG, PNG or WebP file.
  2. Leave the quality slider at about 70%, or raise it for a closer match to the original.
  3. Click Compress and compare the before and after.
  4. Download the smaller file.

If you need an exact limit, the target pages accept any size in kilobytes, so you can open the 200KB page and type 1,800 for a 2 MB limit. Everything runs in your browser, so your photo is never uploaded.

One thing to know: a JPG stays a JPG, but a PNG is saved as WebP to keep transparency. If the place you are uploading to accepts only JPG, convert the PNG to JPG before compressing.

Way 2: Reduce the pixel dimensions

Resizing removes pixels you will never see. On a Mac, open the photo in Preview and choose Tools → Adjust Size. On Windows, open it in Paint and choose Resize. Most phone editors have a similar option. For viewing on screens, a width of roughly 1,200 to 2,000 pixels is plenty. Keep larger sizes only if you plan to print. After resizing, compress the result for an even smaller file.

Way 3: Change the format

Format matters more than most people expect. An iPhone photo saved as HEIC will not open in many places, so convert it to JPG first. A photograph saved as PNG is usually much bigger than the same photo as a JPG, because PNG keeps every pixel. Switching to JPG makes it smaller, although it removes transparent backgrounds. Our format guide explains which to choose.

Way 4: Crop out what you do not need

Less picture means fewer pixels to store. If the subject fills only part of the frame, cropping tightly can cut the size noticeably and often makes the photo look better too.

Way 5: Use your phone’s share options

When you attach a photo to an email on an iPhone, the Mail app usually offers size choices such as Small, Medium and Large. Many Android phones have a similar resize option when sharing. It is quick for one or two photos, but you control the final size less precisely than with a compressor.

Which method should you use?

  • In a hurry, or one photo: compress it online.
  • Photo is huge, such as 8 MB or more: resize first, then compress.
  • Won’t open or upload: check the format and convert to JPG.
  • A few photos for email: try your phone’s share options, or compress them together.

Tips for the best result

  • Work from the original. Every round of compression removes a little more detail, so avoid compressing the same copy repeatedly.
  • Check the result. Zoom in on faces and text before you send or upload.
  • Keep your originals. Save the smaller version as a separate copy.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I make a 5 MB photo 1 MB?

Resize it to a smaller width, then compress it with a target near 1,000 KB. Combining the two usually gets there without an obvious loss of quality.

Will making a photo smaller reduce its quality?

Some detail is removed, but at moderate settings the difference is usually hard to see on a screen. Compare the original and the smaller copy.

Why are my phone photos so large?

Modern phone cameras capture very high pixel counts, so each photo is several megabytes. Most uses, such as email and forms, need far less.

What is the difference between MB and KB?

Both measure file size. A megabyte is about 1,000 kilobytes, so a 2 MB photo is roughly 2,000 KB.
