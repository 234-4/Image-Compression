JPG vs PNG vs WebP: Which Image Format Should You Use?

Published October 27, 2026 · 5 min read

Open “Save as” on almost any image and you get the same short list: JPG, PNG, WebP. They all produce a picture, but they behave very differently. Choose badly and you end up with a bloated file, blurry text, or a form that refuses your upload. Here is how to choose well.

The quick answer

FormatBest forTransparencyCompression
JPGPhotographsNoLossy
PNGLogos, screenshots, graphics with textYesLossless
WebPWebsites, photos and graphicsYesLossy or lossless

JPG: the all-round choice for photos

JPG throws away detail the eye is unlikely to miss, which keeps photos small. Every device, browser, email app and upload form understands it. The trade-offs are that it cannot have a transparent background, and that sharp edges and small text can look fuzzy. Re-saving a JPG again and again also wears it down a little each time.

PNG: sharp and exact

PNG keeps every pixel exactly as it was, so it is the usual choice for logos, icons, screenshots and anything with crisp lines or text. It also supports transparency. The cost is size: a PNG photo can be several times larger than the same photo as a JPG.

WebP: usually the smallest

WebP can work in lossy or lossless mode and supports transparency, so it can stand in for both JPG and PNG. At similar visual quality its files are often noticeably smaller, which is why it is popular for websites and page speed. All current major browsers support it. The catch is that some older software, email tools and upload forms still do not accept it.

Which one should you use?

  • A photo for a website: WebP, or JPG if you want maximum compatibility.
  • A photo for an online form, email or print shop: JPG. It is the safest choice.
  • A logo or icon with a transparent background: PNG, or WebP for the web.
  • A screenshot with text: PNG. JPG tends to smudge small letters.
  • A graphic you will keep editing: PNG, so nothing is lost between saves.

How our compressor treats each format

A JPG stays a JPG. A PNG is saved as WebP so that transparency is kept while the file shrinks. If a browser cannot create WebP, the image is saved as a JPG with a white background instead. If your upload form only takes JPG, start from a JPG, or convert your PNG first. Our guide to compressing images for online forms explains this in more detail.

Common mistakes

  • Saving photos as PNG. The file gets large with no visible benefit.
  • Saving screenshots as JPG. Text looks soft and surrounded by faint noise.
  • Converting a JPG to PNG to “restore” quality. The detail that was removed does not come back, and the file just gets bigger.
  • Editing and re-saving the same JPG repeatedly. Keep the original and export a fresh copy each time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is WebP better than JPG?

For websites, WebP files are often smaller at similar quality, so it is usually the better choice. JPG is still the safer choice when the file has to work everywhere, such as forms, email and older software.

Does converting PNG to JPG lose quality?

Yes. JPG is lossy, so some detail is removed, and any transparent areas become a solid background color. Keep your PNG as the original.

Can I get quality back by converting JPG to PNG?

No. Converting only changes the container. The detail that was already removed stays gone, and the PNG will usually be larger.

Does WebP work everywhere?

It works in all current major browsers, but some older programs and upload forms do not accept it. If a form rejects your file, convert it to JPG.
